# Sources and search discipline

The whole skill is worthless if the sources are junk. Read this before every
search pass.

## The four source classes

Not all evidence is the same weight, and treating a press release like a
practitioner's post is how a read goes quietly wrong. Sort every source into one
of these before you use it.

**Class A — a real person with something at stake.** Someone speaking from their
own experience, dated, in a specific place.
- Essays, Substack posts, personal blogs, newsletters written under a byline
- Forum and comment threads — including the replies, which are usually better
  than the post
- Review text where someone describes what actually happened
- Podcast and video transcripts, comment sections
- Reporting where a named person argues a position rather than aggregating one

*Use freely. This is the material.*

**Class B — an expert reading, already interpreted.** The analyst roster,
researchers, named strategists. Someone with a career and a thesis.

*Use in its own section, never merged into the field. "People are saying X" and
"a named strategist argues X" are different claims, and quietly converting the
second into the first is the most respectable-looking way this skill could lie
to you.*

**Class C — a party with an interest.** Vendor blogs, product marketing, "we
tested 10 tools" posts by someone selling an eleventh, sponsored research,
press releases and newswire.

*Usable only as evidence of what a market is claiming about itself — never as
evidence that a claim is true, and never to raise a finding's strength. If the
only support for a finding is Class C, the finding is `thin` at best.*

**Class D — text nobody meant.** SEO listicles, AI-written filler, content farms,
undated anything.

*Not a source. Don't quote it, don't count it, don't summarise it.*

**The tell for D is always the same:** nobody speaking, no date, no first person,
no stakes, no particulars, no mess. If a page could have been written about any
topic by anyone at any time, it isn't a source. Real people are specific and
badly punctuated.

## When the results are all C and D

This is the most likely thing to happen in any commercially active category, and
it is what sank the previous version of this skill: three searches returned
nothing but vendor pages, and the read carried on anyway.

**Change route. Don't summarise garbage.**

If a pass comes back mostly Class C and D:

1. **Say so out loud** in the read — "searching the category's own words returned
   only people selling into it" is itself a finding about how saturated a space
   is.
2. **Drop the category vocabulary entirely** and search the vernacular from the
   map (see [read.md](read.md), move 2).
3. **Change platform, not just wording.** Move to the routes in
   [where-to-search.md](where-to-search.md) that index people rather than pages.
4. **Go at it sideways** — the adjacent territory, the moment before, the
   complaint rather than the product.

A read assembled from Class C sources looks exactly like a real read. That's why
this rule is here.

**Anonymous and pseudonymous voices count.** A throwaway Reddit account writing
honestly about something it wouldn't put its name to is often the *best* source
you'll find — people say things under a handle they'd never say under a byline.
What matters is that a person is speaking from experience, dated, in a specific
place. What doesn't count is unattributed *institutional* text: no author
because there is no author.

## The four passes

**Pass one breaks your own map.** Before looking for material, test whether the
room you named is the room that exists. Search for the rival map from
[read.md](read.md) move 2 — the other audience this could be for, the other
moment it could bite. If the evidence points at the rival, revise the map now,
while it's cheap.

**Pass two uses their words, from the map.** Not the category's words — those
belong to the people selling into it, and searching them returns Class C and D
almost exclusively in any active market. Use the vernacular the map gave you:
the complaint, the moment, the adjacent territory. This is where the material
is. If it returns only vendor pages, you're still using category vocabulary
without noticing — go back and get further from it.

**Pass three tests what the subject itself claims.** When someone points you at
a page or a product, its copy is making testable assertions about what the
audience wants. "Like having three tools in one" claims people want fewer tools.
"Most natural agent" claims people want human-sounding AI. **Search those claims
as questions.** They're free hypotheses that didn't come from you, which makes
them the best correction available to your own framing.

**Pass four tries to prove yourself wrong.** Mandatory. Once a finding is
forming, search for its opposite: if the read is heading toward "people are
exhausted by X", go looking for people who still love X. If a word looks
curdled, look for it being used sincerely, recently. Report what that pass
found — including when it found nothing, which is itself the strongest version
of the finding.

**One more thing, at the end: re-read your own quotes.** Once themes exist, go
back through everything you collected and check it against each theme. A quote
gets filed under the first idea it seemed to fit and then stays there. In the
run that produced this rule, the single best piece of evidence for the user's
actual concern was sitting in the notes, filed under the wrong finding, and was
never used.

Without this pass, the skill degenerates into finding a few spicy quotes and
calling them a room. That is the most likely way this goes wrong in practice,
and it will look completely convincing when it does.

**Pass three has to leave a trail, or it didn't happen.** Every finding records
three things, in the saved document:

- **the disconfirming query** you actually ran, verbatim
- **the best counter-evidence** it turned up — the strongest thing you found
  against your own claim, quoted, not characterised
- **what it did to the claim:** `strengthened` (searched hard, found nothing
  against it) / `narrowed` (true, but for fewer people or in fewer places than
  I first thought) / `contested` (real pushback exists, both sides reported) /
  `killed` (finding dropped)

If every finding in a read comes back `strengthened`, treat that as a warning
about the searching, not a triumph. Real cultural claims almost always narrow
under pressure. What that looks like:

- went in: *"people are exhausted by X"*
- came out: *"people who've already tried X twice are exhausted by it;
  first-timers are still enthusiastic"*

The second is worth far more — it's actionable, it names which audience you're
talking about, and it's the version that survives contact with a real user.
**A read where nothing narrowed is a read that didn't push.**

## Where to look

The routes live in **[where-to-search.md](where-to-search.md)**, with a
last-verified date on each. Read it before you start.

**Treat that file as hints, never as truth.** Doors close without warning —
Reddit was the richest source in this skill's first version and is now walled at
every entrance. Every route gets tested live during the run, and the read
reports which ones worked *today*, not which ones the file says work.

**And check the user's own list.** If a curated source file exists (see
[where-to-search.md](where-to-search.md)), read it first — a hand-kept list of
people worth reading beats searching, in any category polluted enough to matter.
It is a starting shelf, not evidence: anything taken from it still gets fetched
live and dated.

**The "200 but empty" trap.** A page can return 200 and still be a JavaScript
shell. If a fetch comes back suspiciously small — a few kilobytes where you'd
expect tens — you got the shell, not the page. Treat it as a failed fetch and
find another way in. **This applies to the subject you were pointed at as much
as to a field source**, and it has already caused one bad read.

Fetch the promising ones and read them properly. A search snippet is not a
source; it's a pointer to one.

## Dates and windows

- **Anchor every search to today's actual date**, and **print the window in the
  output**. If it isn't written down, nothing forces you to notice a source that
  fell outside it.
- **Default window: last three months** — but the window belongs to the topic,
  not to the skill. A tooling category turns over in weeks; a professional
  identity shifts over years. Pick it deliberately and say why.
- Exception: the "what curdled" question needs a **two-year** look-back, because
  you can only see a word turn by comparing it to itself.
- **Anything outside the window is labelled outside the window** at the point of
  use, every time, not just in a table.
- Record the date on every quote. A quote without a date can't carry a shelf
  life, and a finding without a shelf life is unusable.

**Recency breaks ties.** When two sources disagree and one is newer, the newer
one wins — and **the change itself becomes a finding**. "This was true in
January and isn't now" is more valuable than either source alone, and it's the
kind of thing only a dated read can see.

Fast-moving spaces punish stale reads hard. In one read, a named practitioner
dated a mood shift to a sixty-day window — meaning a read done two months
earlier would have been confidently wrong, with nothing about it looking wrong.

## How much is enough

### What counts as one community

The thresholds below are meaningless without this, so count strictly:

- **One community** = one subreddit, one forum, one Discord, one comment
  section, one publication's readership. Two subreddits are two communities.
- **One thread is not one community.** It's one conversation inside one.
- **One platform** = Reddit, X, HN, YouTube, a review site. Three subreddits are
  three communities but still **one platform** — and people on one platform
  talk like each other. Cross-platform agreement is worth far more than
  cross-subreddit agreement.
- The same person posting in three places is still one voice. Check handles.

### The strength labels

**These are assigned by counting, not by judgement.** The label is the first
thing an agent will fudge, because picking "solid" licenses a better sentence.
So the tests below are mechanical, and several of them are *mandatory* — they
override anything the evidence feels like.

- **Solid** — three or more opened Class A sources, across at least two distinct
  communities *on different platforms*, all inside the window, and no more than
  half the evidence from any single thread.
- **Thin** — two sources, or three from one community. **Mandatory** when the
  support is mostly Class C, whatever the count.
- **Single-thread** — **mandatory** when it all came from one conversation, one
  community, or one publication. Say so in the finding: "one thread, twelve
  replies, no sign of it elsewhere."
- **Contested** — **mandatory** when credible sources point different ways, or
  when the sentiment splits by audience. Report both sides; never average them
  into a verdict.
- **Can't tell** — below thin. A real answer, and always better than a confident
  guess.

**Three rules that stop label laundering:**

1. **A finding cannot be `solid` if its strongest quote is single-thread.** You
   can't build a solid claim on one conversation plus corroboration that doesn't
   actually say the same thing.
2. **Class C sources never raise a label.** They can only lower it.
3. **The label travels to the sentence.** It governs the verb in the report (see
   the sentence rule in [read.md](read.md)), not just a note beside the finding.
   A hedge that stays in the evidence paragraph while the recommendation
   underneath is written as an instruction has done nothing.

### Say whose room you actually read

Every platform is a self-selected population, and some are wildly skewed. Any
finding where one route supplied most of the evidence carries a warning naming
the skew.

Known skews worth stating: developer forums are cynical about marketing by
temperament; open federated platforms currently over-represent people who are
actively hostile to AI; newswire and news aggregators over-represent press
releases; newsletter platforms over-represent the considered, published register
and under-represent the private gripe.

"Three subreddits are three communities but still one platform" is the general
form of this: **cross-platform agreement is worth far more than cross-community
agreement inside one platform.**

### The soft stop

There is already a hard stop for when nothing could be opened. This is the
quieter failure: sources opened, but none of them are any good.

If the only material you found is Class C and D, or everything came from one
skewed room, **downgrade the read rather than finishing it**. Say plainly that
the field was too thin to read, report what the adjacent questions returned,
and hand over **questions worth investigating** instead of themes. Do not
produce implications for someone's product off a weak field.

A weak read, labelled weak, is useful. A weak read dressed as a strong one is
the worst thing this skill can produce, because nothing about it looks wrong.

**One thread supports one finding, maximum.** If you find yourself drawing three
separate findings out of the same conversation, you're not reading a room,
you're reading a thread closely. Go and corroborate elsewhere or collapse them
into one.

A search that returned nothing gets reported as a search that returned nothing.
Silence in a space is information.

### When the central question comes back empty

Silence on a side question is a finding. **Silence on the thing the read is
actually about is a stop.**

If the question at the heart of this read returns no in-window sources from real
people — nobody is discussing it, or every result is vendors selling into it —
then you do not fill the gap with inference. Inference dressed as a finding is
the failure this whole skill exists to prevent, and a thin field is exactly when
it's most tempting.

Instead:

1. Say plainly that the field is silent, and show the queries you ran so they
   can see you looked properly.
2. Report whatever the *adjacent* questions did return, labelled as adjacent.
3. **Ask them one question:** what do you know about this that search won't
   return — something a customer said, something you've seen, something from a
   call. They have context the field doesn't, and this is the moment it's worth
   more than anything you could find.
4. Say what would actually answer it, if research can't. Usually a handful of
   user tests.

This is the one place the skill stops and asks. Take it — a question here is
worth more than five confident paragraphs.

### Voices that aren't people

The window you're searching is full of text that no one meant. Before a source
counts, check for:

- **Astroturf and marketing in disguise** — accounts that only ever post about
  one product, review bursts clustered on one date, unnatural repetition of a
  brand's exact phrasing.
- **Coordinated outrage** — a spike that traces back to one post or one
  personality. That's an event, not a mood. It can look identical to a genuine
  shift and it decays in weeks.
- **AI-generated filler** — fluent, unspecific, no stakes, no particulars, no
  mess. Real people are specific and badly punctuated.

None of this is always detectable. When something looks organised rather than
felt, say so in the finding rather than dropping it silently — "this reads
coordinated" is useful information about a space.

## Quoting

- **Short and verbatim.** A few words to a sentence or two. Don't tidy the
  grammar; the roughness is the register.
- **Never stitch fragments from different people into one apparent voice.** Two
  half-quotes joined together is a fabricated consensus.
- **Keep the quote and your reading of it visibly separate.** The quote is
  evidence; what it means is your inference and gets labelled as such.
- **Don't quote someone into the opposite of what they meant.** If the
  surrounding context flips it, either include the context or drop the quote.
- **Every quote carries its context**, recorded alongside it: where it sat in
  the conversation, whether the speaker was agreeing or arguing, and why it
  supports the finding. A quote without its stance is a quote that can be
  pointed anywhere.

### Quoting ordinary people

These are members of the public, not public figures, and many are writing about
things they wouldn't want followed back to them.

- **Never include real names, usernames or handles** for ordinary people. The
  platform and the date are enough — "a commenter on r/<sub>, June 2026".
  Named public writers and the analyst roster are the exception; credit them
  fully.
- **Don't quote from spaces built for safety** — recovery, illness, abuse,
  addiction, or anywhere people are clearly writing in confidence. Read them to
  understand, characterise what you learned in your own words, and don't lift
  the sentences.
- **Never quote anything identifying** — a story specific enough to find the
  person is not anonymised by removing the handle.

## Honesty rules

- **Report thin fields as thin.** "Four real voices, three of them in one
  thread" is a valid finding — it tells the user this space is quiet, which is
  itself information.
- **Don't launder a single loud thread into "people are saying".** Say how many,
  and where.
- **Separate what you saw from what you infer.** Inference is allowed and often
  the most valuable part — it just has to be labelled as inference.
- **One community is not the world.** If everything came from one subreddit,
  say that the read is one subreddit deep.
- **Never fill a gap with a plausible trend.** A hallucinated cultural finding
  is worse than no skill at all, because nothing about it looks wrong.
- **Say whose culture you actually read.** Searching in English returns
  English-speaking, mostly online, mostly younger, mostly Western culture. If
  the product is for somewhere or someone else, that's a stated limit on the
  whole read, not a footnote — and the parts of a culture that live offline,
  or in a language you didn't search, are invisible to this method entirely.

## The hard stop

Fetching depends on what's installed. The order to try, and what's currently
working, is in [where-to-search.md](where-to-search.md) — check it before you
spend searches.

**A finding requires a source you actually opened.** A search snippet is a
pointer, not evidence. If you couldn't fetch the page, you can note that the
result exists and what its date was — you cannot build a finding on it.

**The stop condition.** If search and fetch are unavailable, or every attempt
fails, or you finish the passes without a single opened in-window source:

- Say plainly that the read was **not completed**, and why.
- Hand over the dated baseline alone, labelled as out of date.
- Produce **nothing else** — no findings, no language calls, no alternatives,
  no shelf lives, no "so what". Not a hedged version either.
- Don't save a document. A half-read that gets saved becomes tomorrow's
  baseline and quietly launders memory into evidence.

**Partial failure** — some sources opened, some didn't, one platform
unreachable — is not a stop. Carry on, and say in the output exactly which
rooms you couldn't get into. A read with a named blind spot is useful. A read
with an unnamed one is dangerous.
