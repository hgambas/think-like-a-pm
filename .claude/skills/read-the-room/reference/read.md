# The full read

**Seven moves, in order.** Moves 2 and 3 are the ones that decide whether the
rest is worth anything — they are where the last version of this skill failed.

Read [sources.md](sources.md) before you search, and
[where-to-search.md](where-to-search.md) for the routes that currently work.

---

## Move 1 — Work out what you're being handed

Three kinds of ask arrive here. They need different first steps and the same
delivery.

**A subject** — a URL, a page, a product, a whole position. They've pointed at
a thing.

**A territory** — "how do people feel about X right now", "what's the mood
around Y". No artifact, just a topic.

**One word or one line** — a name, a tagline, a button label. "Does this still
land?" Same work, smaller. See below.

Take the branch that fits. If a page *and* a broad question both arrive, run
the subject branch, then carry on.

### When the question is one word or one line

Do not run a different, faster method. Run this one, scoped small — the whole
failure this skill guards against is a confident sentence built on one person's
offhand remark, and a "quick" mode is where that happens.

What changes: you search **the actual phrase** and its close cousins as people
really write them, not the concept. You're asking whether it's in use and by
whom, whether it's curdled into a joke, whether it's so saturated it now says
nothing, and whether it collides with a meaning from somewhere else that
matters.

The answer is one of five, said plainly:

- **Lands** — in use, in their register, not saturated.
- **Dated** — reads as a few years ago. Say what replaced it.
- **Saturated** — accurate, but everyone says it, so it says nothing. Name the
  crowded position.
- **Collides** — it means something else somewhere that matters. Say where, and
  how badly.
- **Can't tell** — the field was too thin. A real answer, and better than a
  confident guess.

If it isn't "lands", offer two or three alternatives **drawn from phrasings you
actually found**, each with the quote it came from. Never invent a replacement
out of thin air; the entire value is that it came from a real mouth.

Then say how long the answer holds. "Safe for a year" and "this will read as
dated by spring" are different answers and change what someone would commit to.

### If they pointed at a subject: capture it before you judge it

**The words on the thing are evidence, and they get the same discipline as a
quote from a stranger.** The failure this prevents is real and it has happened:
an agent fetched a landing page, got a truncated response that silently dropped
the headline, analysed the middle of the page, and called it the positioning.

1. **Fetch it and check you got all of it.** A page that comes back at a few
   kilobytes when you'd expect tens is a shell, not a page — the same
   "200 but empty" trap that applies to field sources applies here. Try a second
   route before believing what you have.
2. **Transcribe it verbatim**: headline, subhead, every section heading, body
   copy, button labels, nav, footer, the browser tab title and the meta
   description. Tab titles and meta descriptions are what people see in search
   results and pasted links — they are positioning, even though they aren't on
   the page.
3. **Ask what this is one of.** A URL with a query string, a `/v2`, a variant
   name, an A/B parameter is telling you it's one of several. Go and look at
   what it's a variant of, what sits around it — pricing, FAQ, the main page —
   and which of those the audience actually meets first. Say what differs
   between them; the difference is often the most interesting thing you'll find.

Then stop looking at it. **You are not auditing their product.** If your output
could have been written by reading their page and thinking hard, you've written
the wrong document. The capture exists so that later, when the field tells you
something, you can hold it against the exact words.

### Whichever branch: name the thing and the two rooms

- **The thing:** what it is and what it lets someone do, in the words you'd use
  to a friend rather than the words on the homepage.
- **The category** it gets filed under.
- **The topic** it's really about, which is usually not the category.
- **The two rooms:** the category itself, and the emotional territory — what's
  happening to this person in the minute *before* they'd reach for it. The
  second room is where the useful material lives.

## Move 2 — Build a working map, and label it a guess

**This is the move that replaces searching the category's own vocabulary.**

Here's why it exists. A category's keywords are written mostly by the people
selling into that category. Search "B2B lead qualification tools" and you find
vendors, SEO listicles and AI-written filler — not one person with the problem.
The people who *have* the feeling don't use those words at all. So the old
approach of "search industry words, then harvest the real phrasings from what
comes back" collapses the moment a category is commercially saturated: nothing
real comes back, so there's nothing to harvest.

Instead, work out where the feeling lives, and search *that*.

Write a short map:

- **Who this actually bites** — a role specific enough that you could find their
  rooms. "Marketers" is a job title. "The person who owns the demo form and gets
  blamed when sales says the leads are rubbish" is a room.
- **The moment it bites** — what just happened, what they were doing, what
  they've already tried.
- **What they call it** — the words they'd use to a colleague, which are almost
  never the category's words.
- **What they say they like** near this, and **what they're pushing away from**.
- **Adjacent territories** carrying the same feeling under a different name.
- **The search terms this gives you** — derived from the map, not from the
  category.

**Now the part that stops this becoming a more sophisticated way to guess.**

The map is built from your own out-of-date knowledge. If you write it, then
search to confirm it, you have automated confirmation bias and the result will
look completely convincing. So:

- **Label it a working hypothesis, not a finding.** Every line carries why you
  inferred it and what would prove it wrong.
- **Name at least one rival map.** Who *else* could this be for? A founder
  doing their own sales, an agency, an ops hire, the buyer rather than the
  buyer's company. Write the second one down.
- **Search to break it before you search to fill it.** Your first queries test
  whether the room you named is the room that exists.
- **Watch for the wrong-map alarms.** Live sources using different role names
  than your map; people discussing a different moment than you predicted;
  searches returning only vendor pages; quotes clustering around a general
  ideology rather than this buying situation. Any of those and the map is
  probably wrong — revise it and say so.
- **Report what happened to it.** The read says either "my starting map changed
  because…" or "my starting map held, and here's what confirmed it."

## Move 3 — Say where you're pointing it, then go

Show the map. **Do not wait for a reply** — post it and start searching in the
same turn.

**Frame it as a decision about where to look, not a request for approval.**
"Have I got this right?" hands your job back to them and gets skimmed. Say what
you think is worth investigating and why it's the interesting question, and let
them push back.

The line between confident and overreaching runs through the *object* of the
sentence, not its tone:

- **Be decisive about what you're going to look at.** "The room I think is worth
  reading first is X, because Y." "The words I'll search aren't the category's —
  they're these."
- **Be modest about what you'll find.** You haven't looked yet. "People care
  about X" and "the cultural tension is Y" are findings, and you don't have any.

> **Where I'm pointing this**
>
> I think the live question here is less about [the category] and more about
> [the territory] — so the first room I'm reading is [who], at the moment
> [when].
>
> The words I'll actually search aren't "[category term]", because that phrase
> mostly returns people selling it. They're [the vernacular from the map].
>
> The other reading is that this is really about [rival map]. I'll test both and
> report which one the evidence supports.
>
> Starting now — say if I'm pointed at the wrong thing.

If a correction arrives mid-read, fold it in and say what it changed. If nothing
arrives, the read stands on the map and the report says so.

## Move 4 — Go to the field

Live search, every time, never from memory. Follow the routes and the discipline
in [sources.md](sources.md) and [where-to-search.md](where-to-search.md).

Default window is the last three months, **and the window varies by topic** — a
tooling category turns over in weeks, a professional identity shifts over years.
Say which window you used and why. Extend to two years for the "what curdled"
question, which needs the look-back to see a word turn.

Take these to the field. Report the ones that came back empty as empty.

**On language**
1. How do people describe this experience in their own words right now — the
   actual phrasings, not your paraphrase?
2. What did people say two years ago that now gets mocked?
3. What's the word an insider uses that an outsider wouldn't know to use?

**On mood**
4. What is this world arguing about, and who are the camps?
5. What are people actively pushing away from?
6. What is everyone clearly feeling but nobody says out loud?
7. What promise are people tired of hearing?

**Question 6 is the one most likely to produce fiction.** Only answer it from a
moment you can point at — a confession that gets hundreds of agreeing replies, a
joke that only lands if everyone already feels the thing. If you can't quote
where it surfaced, say the question came back empty. That's allowed and honest.

**On movement**
8. What's newly sayable that wasn't?
9. What shipped near this recently, and how did real people react, as opposed to
   how it was covered?
10. Which adjacent conversation is this one bleeding into?

**Question 9 is not competitor analysis.** You want what people said back — the
jokes, the complaints, the thing everyone repeated. If your answer would fit in
a strategy deck, you've drifted. "Near" means near in feeling, not in category.

**Collect actual quotes**, short and verbatim, with a source, a date and the
speaker's stance. A paraphrase is worth nothing — the phrasing *is* the finding.

**Every finding carries its evidence label**, assigned by the mechanical rules in
[sources.md](sources.md), not by how strong it feels.

## Move 5 — Consult the roster

Required, and only after the field passes. Read [analysts.md](analysts.md), pick
the two or three whose beat actually covers this, fetch what they've published
inside the window, and read them.

Field first, always — their frames are good enough to decide what you notice,
and you want to notice on your own first.

Their thinking goes in **its own section**. Credit at the point of use, and
credit the *frame* as loudly as the words. Where an analyst and the field
disagree, say so — it's usually the best line in the read.

If none of them work this beat, say so. That's honest and it shows where the
roster has a hole.

## Move 6 — Report

**The conversation is the deliverable.** Not a file, not a template.

### How it has to read

Assume the reader hasn't read your sources, doesn't read code, and is going to
skim it once between two meetings.

- **Whole answer first, short.** If they asked a question, answer it in the
  first three lines.
- **Plain, everyday words.** No jargon, no category vocabulary, nothing that
  needs a glossary. If you can't say it plainly you don't understand it yet.
- **Every confident sentence carries its evidence directly underneath** — one or
  two sentences, quoted, dated, attributed. Not a citation to go and check: the
  actual words.
- **Length is capped by evidence, not by ambition.** Two or three themes. If it's
  getting long, cut themes, never cut evidence.

### The shape

1. **The answer** — what they asked, answered, in a few lines.
2. **What moved in my map** — one or two lines. Did the starting guess survive
   contact with the field?
3. **The evidence** — the quotes. Verbatim, dated, real people. Before the
   conclusions, because it's the reason to believe them.
4. **Two or three themes**, most important first. Each one runs:
   - **What people are doing or saying**, with the quote and its evidence label
   - **What that means here** — the connection to their thing
   - **The decision it creates** — the choice this puts in front of them
   - **What not to read into it** — the boundary
5. **What I couldn't reach** — rooms, platforms, languages, and what's missing
   because of it.

### The sentence rule

The evidence label governs the verb. This is not decoration; it is the thing
that stops one person's offhand remark becoming a rule.

| Label | The strongest sentence you may write |
|---|---|
| Solid | "This leads me to think X is worth weighing." |
| Thin | "Two people said this, which might mean X." |
| Single-thread | "One person said this. It may not generalise, but it raises X." |
| Contested | "There's a real argument both ways, and it turns on X." |
| Can't tell | "The field was quiet on this. Here's what would answer it." |

**Any sentence that recommends something inherits the label of the claim it
rests on.** A thin finding cannot produce a confident instruction.

### Implications, not instructions

You are handing over what the world is doing and what that puts in play. **You
are not authoring their product.**

- **Don't write:** "Kill that line." "Move this to the hero." "Rename it X."
- **Do write:** what the finding makes risky, what choice it forces, and what
  they'd be trading either way.

The failure on the other side is just as real — a beautifully-written read that
leaves someone asking "so what?" is worthless. The middle is **decision
pressure**: name the choice, name what each direction costs, and let them make
it. If they ask for drafted options, give them; unasked, don't.

### Shelf life

Every theme is stamped, **from the dates you collected, never from a feeling**:

- **Six-week fashion** — evidence clustered inside about a month, one or two
  platforms, traceable to a specific moment, launch or personality.
- **One-year mood** — dated evidence spanning three months or more, three or
  more communities, at least two platforms, no single origin event.
- **Multi-year shift** — all of the above, *plus* two-year look-back evidence
  showing the change, *plus* at least one source where people describe **doing**
  something differently, not only talking differently. Behaviour is the
  qualifier.

If the dates don't support a stamp, say the shelf life is unknown. That's a real
answer; a guessed one gets acted on.

If a ship date is known, do the arithmetic out loud — a finding that won't
outlive the build is a distraction, and say so.

### The crowded position

If the field shows everyone in a space has moved the same way, say it. That's
not a trend, it's a crowded position, and the case for standing somewhere else
is worth making. A read that only ever points toward consensus makes everything
look the same, which is a real failure mode of this skill.

## Move 7 — Ask before you save anything

**Never write a file as a side effect of doing the work.**

When the read is delivered, ask once, plainly: *is this worth keeping so you can
refer back to it later?*

- **Only on a yes** do you write anything.
- **Name it so anyone can tell what it is.** `docs/culture-research/<the
  project, page or thing>.md` — the name comes from what they're working on, not
  from a slug you invent.
- **Use the shape in [document.md](document.md)** so a later re-read can diff
  against it.
- **On a no, or no answer, nothing gets written**, and you say in one line that
  `refresh` will have nothing to diff against.

## Pitfalls

- **Writing it from memory.** The fatal one. No search, no read.
- **Searching the category's own words.** They belong to the people selling it.
  Move 2 exists to get you out of that.
- **Confirming your own map.** A map you never tried to break is a guess wearing
  a research costume.
- **Reading only the category.** The emotional territory is where real people
  are.
- **Paraphrasing the vernacular.** The exact words are the deliverable.
- **Summarising garbage.** If the results are vendor pages and SEO filler,
  change route — don't write up what you found.
- **Rigour arriving as volume.** A long, hedged, jargon-heavy report is a failed
  report, however good the research underneath it was.
- **Treating the informant as ground truth.** They're one person in one bubble,
  and so are you.
