# From signal to product

The bridge. A cultural finding and a product change are three steps apart, and
handing someone the first and the last is why good research reads as a leap.

## The four layers

Every theme in a read runs top to bottom, and every layer is traceable to the
one above it.

**1. Signal** — what the world is doing, with the quote and the date. Not your
reading of it; the thing itself. Carries its evidence label.

**2. Theme** — what that means this product has to stand for. One sentence, a
stance, arguable.

**3. Where it lands** — which surfaces and moments this theme governs. Not one
screen; the class of places it applies.

**4. Decision pressure** — the choice this forces, and what each direction
trades away. Plus the boundary: what this evidence does *not* support.

**Why the middle matters: a theme is reusable, a change is single-use.** Hand
someone a change and they get one edit. Hand them the theme underneath it and
they can apply it to the landing page, the emails, the naming and next quarter —
and, more valuable, they can tell what *not* to build.

## Layer 4 is where the last version went wrong, in both directions

The old fourth layer asked for "the specific edits — named screen, named line."
That made the skill author product decisions it had no standing to make: one
person's offhand remark about a word became "never use that word."

The overcorrection is just as bad. A tasteful cultural memo that leaves someone
asking "so what?" is worthless, and "a beautiful read that changes nothing" is a
real failure of this skill.

**The middle is decision pressure. Name the choice, don't make it.**

- **Not:** "Kill that line." "Move this to the hero." "Rename it X."
- **Not:** "Interesting how people feel about qualification." *(so what?)*
- **But:** "This makes leading on *qualified* risky, because the sharper
  complaints aren't about the word — they're about leads being called qualified
  when sales still has to redo discovery. The choice that creates: whether the
  page proves the standard, or just claims the outcome. **What this doesn't
  support:** that the word has to go."

Four things in that unit, and the last one is not optional:

1. **What people are doing or saying** — with the quote
2. **What it means here** — the connection to their thing
3. **The decision it creates** — the choice, and the cost either way
4. **What not to read into it** — the boundary the evidence can't cross

Write it as prose, not as four labelled headings. The headings are how a report
gets long, and length is its own failure.

**If they ask for drafted options, give them.** Unasked, hand over the decision,
not the answer.

## What makes a theme a theme

- **It's a stance, not a description.** "People distrust AI agents" describes the
  world. "This product's job is to prove judgement, not capability" takes a
  position.
- **You could believe the opposite.** If no sane person would argue the other
  side, it's a platitude, not a theme.
- **It generates more than one change.** One change means you found a fix and
  reverse-engineered a theme around it.
- **It tells you what to stop doing.** The best themes close doors.
- **It couldn't be pasted into another product's read.** "Be more authentic"
  fits anything, which means it's worth nothing.

**Two or three themes, maximum.** A read with six themes has none — it has a
list, and the user has to do the prioritising you were supposed to do.

## The translation table

This is the actual craft: which kind of cultural signal converts into which kind
of product move. Work down the left column and find the one that matches what
the field returned.

**The field says people don't believe claims in this category.**
→ Don't write better claims. Move the argument out of the copy entirely and into
something the user watches happen. Proof-shaped design, not proof-shaped
sentences. This is the single most common conversion and the one most often
botched by rewriting the headline.

**The field says people fear X.**
→ Demonstrate the opposite; never name the fear. Naming a fear plants it in
people who didn't have it, and reassurance-by-assertion is another claim in a
category that has stopped believing claims.

**The field says people use word A where the industry uses word B.**
→ Relabel. Labels are what people navigate by, and a label in the industry's
dialect marks you as an outsider before anyone reads the second sentence.

**The field says people are exhausted by promise P.**
→ Stop leading with P — even if you deliver P better than anyone. Your position
is somewhere else, and the exhausted promise becomes a supporting detail, not
the headline.

**The field says the thing people actually care about is Q.**
→ Order is an argument. What comes first is a claim about what matters. If Q is
in the middle of your flow, your flow is arguing something you don't believe.
First and last are the only positions that carry weight.

**The field says the real anxiety is Q, but the product's reassurance answers R.**
→ The safety copy is aimed at the wrong fear. Common and invisible from inside —
teams reassure about the thing they know is risky, not the thing users dread.

**The field says everyone in the category does X.**
→ Crowded position. Defaults are statements of belief, so look at what's on by
default and what's featured; that's where conformity hides.

**The field says people do this at moment M.**
→ Check the flow's assumed moment against M. Flows are built around when it was
convenient to imagine the user arriving, not when they actually do.

**The field is silent.**
→ No conversion. Say so, and say what would answer it — usually a handful of
user tests. Do not translate silence into a recommendation.

## Two rules that apply to all of it

**Optional means "this is extra."** You cannot answer someone's primary anxiety
in a branch they might not take. If a theme says something is the main worry,
anything addressing it has to be on the main path.

**Labels are promises, and the gap between a promise and the experience is where
distrust forms.** Every CTA, heading and button is a claim about what happens
next. Check them against what actually happens, not against each other.

## The traceability test

Before a change ships in a report, walk it back up: change → where it lands →
theme → signal → quote. If any link is missing, the change is your taste wearing
research clothes. Cut it.
